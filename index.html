You’re about to sign someone else’s paper, and the risk isn’t only what it says.

It’s also what it leaves out. CounterPaper reads a third-party contract against a playbook, from your side of the table, and hands back a clause-by-clause redline you resolve yourself. It never accepts anything on your behalf.

The problem

A lawyer costs more than the deal is worth.

A freelancer, a five-person agency, a founder signing their first enterprise MSA — all of them are handed the other side’s draft and asked to sign it. Counsel for a review costs a meaningful fraction of the contract value, so they sign it unread, or they walk away from the work.

The clauses that hurt them are not obscure. An automatic renewal with a notice deadline nobody tracks. A liability cap set at one month of fees. A consequential-damages waiver that bars recovery of exactly the losses a vendor failure causes.

And the one that is hardest to see: the protection that was never in the document. You cannot read a clause that isn’t there.

AP-07b Liability Cap Too Low High

Found in the document

“Vendor’s total liability under this Agreement shall not exceed one hundred dollars ($100).”

Suggested rewrite

Liability shall be capped at no less than the greater of (a) twelve (12) months of fees or (b) the direct damages actually incurred.

Why: A liability cap at a nominal amount leaves the client with no meaningful recovery if the vendor fails.

Three decisions that shaped it

The interesting part isn’t the model. It’s what the model isn’t allowed to do.

  1. A hybrid engine, because an audit that changes between runs is worse than no audit

    Early on, the same document run twice returned different classifications. That isn’t a quality bug — it means a reviewer can never calibrate how much to trust a flag, and once they can’t, every flag costs them a full manual read of the clause anyway. The tool adds a step and removes nothing.

    So the findings that can be defined precisely don’t go through a model at all. 34 deterministic detectors carry stable rule ids; the LLM, constrained to a strict schema, handles the clauses where risk depends on reading the whole agreement. A reconciler merges them under a documented precedence, and golden-file regression tests in CI fail the build if output drifts.

    On conflictWho wins, and why
    Overlapping flagThe deterministic finding. It is reproducible and carries a stable rule id.
    LLM-only flagKept. That is where coverage of nuanced clauses comes from.
    Missing clauseThe structural detector. Models are unreliable at noticing absence.
    Risk scoreRecomputed from the merged set, so it tracks what was found instead of drifting.

  2. Absence as a finding

    Most review tools react to text that is present. Five structural detectors look for protective language that should be in the agreement and isn’t — no price-increase cap, no termination for convenience, no data-deletion obligation — and raise it as a flag with no source clause to quote.

    This is the half that changes what a non-lawyer can do with the output, because it is the half they would never have thought to look for.

  3. PII masked before inference, described accurately

    Names, emails, and account identifiers are replaced before any text reaches a model, using regex recognisers alongside spaCy named-entity recognition, with a short-lived vault for unmasking in the reviewer’s own session.

    To be precise about what that buys: this is pseudonymisation, not anonymisation. A contract stripped of names is still identifying from its parties, dates and amounts. It reduces what leaks from a single request and it is not sufficient for a regulated buyer — for them the answer is a deployment model, not a redaction step.

What isn’t here

Scoped, and deliberately not built.

A Microsoft Word add-in

In-document redlining is close to table stakes in this category, and this is a real competitive gap. It was scoped and shelved: building distribution for a product nobody has used twice optimises the wrong thing.

A Slack integration

Same reasoning. The intended shape was an agent living where the work happens; the sequencing argument won.

Autonomous redlining

The model proposes. A human resolves every flag. A wrong flag costs a reviewer a moment; a wrong rewrite that gets accepted quietly becomes a term in a signed contract.

Any claim of traction

No users, no pilots, no customers. The MVP went out to cold contacts who never replied — a distribution failure that was read, at first, as a verdict on the product.
